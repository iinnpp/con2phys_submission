Population spiking complexity was examined from stimulus presentation to trial end. Trial selection used exactly
the same filtered trial table as Q11-Q13. A trial was retained only if trial_start, stim_start, outcome, and
trial_end were all present and strictly ordered as trial_start < stim_start < outcome < trial_end. The final
recorded trial for each mouse, defined as the trial with the maximum trial_id, was excluded. The source table
contained 10,373 trials from 18 mice. The completeness and event-order criteria retained 10,361 trials; seven
final trials had already failed those criteria, and excluding the remaining 11 final trials produced a dataset of
10,350 trials.

For each mouse and brain area, single-unit spikes in the half-open interval from stim_start to trial_end,
[stim_start, trial_end), were converted to binary population rasters. Only complete time bins were retained from
each trial, and trials were concatenated chronologically. The primary analysis used 20 ms bins; 10 and 50 ms bins
were analyzed as temporal-resolution sensitivity checks. Each raster was serialized in time-major order, packed
into bits, and compressed with level-1 DEFLATE, an LZ77-family compressor. Complexity was quantified as the
observed compressed size divided by the compressed size of a rate-matched surrogate raster. Thus, larger values
indicate less compressible population activity relative to independent spike timing with the same per-neuron
binned spike counts.

To prevent areas with more recorded neurons from receiving an advantage, the three areas were matched separately
within each mouse to the size of that mouse's smallest recorded area population. Matched population sizes ranged
from 5 to 32 neurons. Units were randomly subsampled and ordered 100 times. One independently seeded rate-matched
surrogate was generated for every subsample by redistributing each selected neuron's active bins while preserving
its total binned spike count. Complexity was averaged across the 100 subsamples, and the resulting mouse-level
mean for each brain area was used as the independent observation for statistical inference.

Differences among brain areas in the primary 20 ms analysis were tested with a linear mixed-effects model using
brain area as a fixed effect and mouse_id as a random intercept. The model showed an overall effect of brain area
on rate-normalized Lempel-Ziv complexity (F(2, 34) = 4.59, p = 0.0171). Model-estimated mean complexity was 0.9761
for area 1 (95% CI: 0.9586-0.9936), 0.9431 for area 2 (0.9256-0.9606), and 0.9506 for area 3 (0.9331-0.9681).
Those values are given in teh table above.

Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons showed that complexity was higher in area 1 than in area 2. The area 1 minus
area 2 difference was 0.0330 (95% CI: 0.0050 to 0.0610, p = 0.0178). Area 1 had a descriptively higher value than
area 3, but this contrast was not significant after Tukey correction: the area 1 minus area 3 difference was
0.0255 (-0.0025 to 0.0535, p = 0.0799). Areas 2 and 3 did not differ: the area 2 minus area 3 difference was
-0.0075 (-0.0355 to 0.0205, p = 0.7886).

The bin-width sensitivity model retained an overall brain-area effect (F(2, 136) = 20.28, p = 1.97e-08), but
showed neither an overall bin-width effect (p = 0.7778) nor a brain-area-by-bin-width interaction (p = 0.5666).
The descriptive ranking was therefore stable across 10, 20, and 50 ms bins.

Therefore, brain area 1 had the highest descriptive population spiking complexity from stimulus presentation to
trial end. However, it was not significantly higher than both other areas in the prespecified primary 20 ms
analysis.
